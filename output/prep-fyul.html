Visión de Producto y Platform Mindset

1
"At FYUL, our goal is to enable Decorators to go from zero to live in under 24 hours without internal intervention. How do you approach designing self-onboarding experiences for complex B2B supply platforms?" Pregunta de apertura. Marcel quiere ver estructura mental, no entusiasmo.
▼
Tu respuesta
I structure self-onboarding around three pillars. First, standardized entry: templates and real-time API validation so bad data gets rejected before it enters the pipeline. Second, background batch validation: automated checks that process catalog submissions without human review, with retry logic and partial error isolation. Third, guided error handling: when something fails, the Decorator sees the exact row, the exact field, and a clear fix instruction directly in the portal — zero support tickets.

In practice, this means the ops team only touches exceptions, not the default flow. At my last role, this structure reduced Time-to-Live from 6.5 days to 31 hours — an 80% reduction — with no increase in headcount.
Tip del entrevistador: La estructura en tres pilares demuestra pensamiento sistemático. Cierra siempre con el número concreto. Marcel está escuchando si distingues entre «diseñar para el proveedor» vs «diseñar para que ops no necesite intervenir».
¿Cómo te salió?

"How do you balance making the platform simple for non-technical suppliers while maintaining strict data structures for your catalog system?" Testa si entiendes que la UX es una capa encima de un esquema rígido, no una negociación con él.
▼
Tu respuesta
The data schema is non-negotiable — it's what makes the catalog interoperable across FYUL's merchant network. What I do is hide the complexity in the UI layer, not in the data layer.

For non-technical Decorators: guided drag-and-drop, auto-mapping suggestions for common field formats, and progressive disclosure — you only see advanced fields if you need them. For technical Decorators: direct API with full schema documentation, bulk upload via CSV with column mapping tools. Both paths write to the same rigid backend schema. The interface adapts; the data contract never does.

"In a marketplace ecosystem, how do you decide where FYUL sets rigid rules versus where we leave flexibility for suppliers to define their own offers?" Pregunta de platform mindset. Quiere ver que entiendes el modelo de plataforma, no solo el portal de proveedor.
▼

"How do you approach localization, multi-currency, or regional compliance requirements when scaling supplier onboarding globally?" Testa si piensas en internacionalización como arquitectura, no como parche.
▼
Tu respuesta
I design for localization from day one, not as an afterthought. The core principle: separate business logic from locale configuration. Tax rules, currencies, regulatory fields, and compliance requirements are injected via configuration — not hardcoded into the catalog model.

In practice, this means five concrete decisions:
  • Dynamic attribute schemas per region. A Decorator in Germany sees REACH compliance fields and VAT inputs; one in the US sees sales tax fields. The validation engine is identical — only the ruleset changes. Adding a new market is a config change, not a dev cycle.
  • Tax as an independent layer. Decorator prices are always net-of-tax in the catalog. A separate, configurable tax service applies country-specific rules at the price layer. When the EU introduced OSS in 2021, platforms with VAT hardcoded in the catalog model faced major migrations. Platforms with it as a separate layer just updated configuration.
  • Data residency configurable from the start. Onboarding collects sensitive Decorator data — company details, fiscal documents. GDPR requires specific storage and retention rules in Europe that differ from the US. If data residency isn't a configurable parameter from day one, every new market becomes a compliance project.
  • Address validation by country code, not hardcoded regex. A UK postcode validator breaks for Japanese or Brazilian addresses. The fix is a configurable address validation library keyed to country_code. Printful already operates across multiple countries — a Decorator in Japan has a completely different address format that a single regex will silently reject.
  • Catalog data language normalized at ingestion. The portal UI can be in the local language, but catalog data entering the system must be normalized to a base encoding. If product names enter in any charset without normalization, attribute matching and search break. Decorators from any region should publish in a format merchants can consume consistently.
The test I apply: can we onboard a Decorator in a new country by changing configuration only, with zero code changes? If the answer is no, we have localization debt.
Tip del entrevistador: FYUL opera con Printify/Printful en múltiples países — esta pregunta tiene peso real. El ejemplo del OSS/VAT de 2021 es concreto y verificable. No cuentes los cinco puntos enteros; elige dos (VAT layer + dynamic schemas) y menciona que tienes el framework completo si Marcel quiere profundizar.
¿Cómo te salió?

Rigor en Ejecución y PRDs

6
"Our JD emphasizes 'solution-complete PRDs' with no 'discovery disguised as delivery'. How do you ensure your PRDs are fully detailed before handover?" Esta frase viene literalmente de la job description. Espera que la cites.
▼
Tu respuesta
Discovery is a gate, not a phase that runs in parallel to delivery. A PRD doesn't go to engineering until it contains: quantified problem statement with baseline data, user stories with explicit acceptance criteria, data flow diagrams for any pipeline or integration, an edge case matrix with frequency and resolution for each, and a sign-off from the Engineering Manager confirming feasibility before I write a single line of acceptance test.

If I can't answer "what's the acceptance test for this edge case?" then I haven't finished Discovery. Engineering should never have to go back to me mid-sprint for a clarification I should have anticipated.
Tip del entrevistador: Listar los cinco componentes del PRD con nombre específico demuestra que no es retórica. La frase «Engineering should never have to come back to me» es exactamente el contrato que Marcel quiere escuchar.

7
"How do you manage edge cases during feature definition without scope creeping the initial release?" Quiere ver que tienes un criterio de corte claro, no «lo vemos sprint a sprint».
▼
Tu respuesta
I use a frequency-impact matrix. Every edge case gets scored on two axes: how often does it happen (% of transactions), and what's the cost if it fails (revenue impact, ops burden).

The rule: if an edge case affects less than 5% of transactions AND takes more than 30% of estimated dev time, it doesn't go into v1. It gets an operational workaround — a manual process for the ops team — documented in the PRD, with a ticket created immediately for v1.1.

This means I'm not hiding the edge case — I'm explicitly deciding to defer it and own the cost of the interim workaround. That's a product decision, not a gap.
Frequency × Impact Matrix
A — Duplicate SKU on submission
B — Missing CMYK profile
C — Multi-currency price mismatch
D — Unsupported image format
Tip del entrevistador: La distinción «esto es una decisión explícita de product, no un gap» es importante. Marcel quiere que asumas la responsabilidad de diferir, no que lo evites. Si Marcel pregunta por un ejemplo concreto, usa el caso B (CMYK) o D — casos reales del dominio de print-on-demand.

▼
Tu respuesta
I evaluate tech debt by its ROI, not by its urgency. If the refactor has a clear throughput or reliability impact on the supplier activation funnel, it gets prioritized. If it's purely internal cleanliness with no measurable user impact, it goes into the 20% tech capacity budget — important, but not at the expense of committed product milestones.
Tip del entrevistador: La clave es que no dices «sí siempre» ni «no por defecto». Tienes un filtro: ¿impacta una métrica de usuario medible? Si la respuesta es sí, el tech debt es un feature disfrazado.

10
"How do you define acceptance criteria for complex background processes like bulk catalog data pipelines?" Muy técnica. Si dices «que funcione correctamente» es un fail instantáneo.
▼
Tu respuesta
For background pipelines, acceptance criteria are always quantitative, not qualitative. I define four dimensions:
  • Throughput: e.g., process 10,000 SKUs per minute at p95
  • Error tolerance: overall failure rate below 1%; partial errors isolate the bad rows, not the full batch
  • Retry policy: exponential backoff with a maximum of 3 retries before dead-letter queue
  • Observability: structured error logs that include row ID, field name, error type, and a human-readable fix instruction — both for the ops dashboard and for the Decorator portal
A pipeline that silently fails 2% of SKUs and logs nothing is worse than a pipeline with a 5% failure rate that gives Decorators actionable feedback.
Tip del entrevistador: Listar cuatro dimensiones con valores concretos (no rangos vagos) demuestra que has especificado pipelines reales. La frase final sobre observabilidad muestra madurez de producto: el peor bug no es el error, es el error silencioso.

Automatización, IA y Herramientas Internas

11
"Supplier onboarding involves complex catalog validation. How have you used automation or AI/LLMs to replace manual operations tasks in the past?" Quiere un ejemplo concreto con impacto medido. No teoría.
▼
Tu respuesta
The clearest example: catalog taxonomy mapping. Suppliers submit product attributes in dozens of different formats and naming conventions. Previously, an ops team member would manually map each supplier's attributes to our internal taxonomy — that took days per supplier and didn't scale.

I spec'd a solution using LLMs with structured JSON output to auto-map incoming attributes to the internal taxonomy. The model was prompted to return field-level confidence scores; anything below 0.85 flagged for human review. Result: processing time from days to minutes per supplier, and manual intervention reduced by 70%. The 15% of low-confidence mappings that still needed human review were the genuinely ambiguous cases — not the routine ones. Ops quality actually improved because they focused on the hard cases.
Tip del entrevistador: El detalle del «confidence threshold 0.85» demuestra que has implementado esto, no solo leído sobre ello. Y el cierre — ops quality improved porque se enfocaron en los casos difíciles — muestra que entiendes que la automatización debe amplificar al equipo humano, no solo reemplazarlo.

"When an automated onboarding process fails due to bad supplier data, how do you handle exception management and error reporting?" Pregunta operacional. Quiere ver que piensas en el flujo de error con el mismo rigor que el flujo feliz.
▼
Tu respuesta
Partial failure is the norm, not the exception. The system should never block a full batch because of bad rows — it processes the valid data and isolates the errors.

For the Decorator: the portal shows a structured error report with the exact row number, the problematic field, what the expected format is, and a one-line fix instruction. No support ticket needed. For ops: a dashboard that aggregates error types by category — so if 40% of errors this week are «missing CMYK profile», that's a signal to add that validation to the pre-submission checklist, not an individual case to fix. Error patterns become product roadmap signals.
Tip del entrevistador: El cierre — «error patterns become roadmap signals» — muestra que cierras el loop de feedback. Marcel quiere un PM que use los datos operacionales para mejorar el producto, no solo para apagar fuegos.

14
"How do you measure the operational cost savings of a self-service onboarding initiative?" Pregunta de ROI. Quiere una fórmula, no una narrativa.
▼
Tu respuesta
I measure it on two dimensions: cost reduction and revenue acceleration.

Cost reduction: (hours of manual ops work eliminated per supplier) × (number of new suppliers per month) × (hourly ops cost). That's the direct savings.

Revenue acceleration: reducing Time-to-Live means suppliers go live earlier and start generating GMV sooner. If average monthly GMV per active supplier is X, and we compress activation by 4 days, that's 4 days × X × number of new suppliers per month in incremental revenue per cohort.

Most teams only track the first dimension. The second is often larger and more strategic — because it ties supply velocity directly to merchant assortment and revenue.
Tip del entrevistador: La distinción entre las dos dimensiones — y especialmente nombrar que la segunda es más estratégica — demuestra pensamiento comercial, que la JD pide explícitamente («commercially minded»).

16
"If catalog growth is rising but supplier activation rates are dropping, how would you diagnose and address the issue?" Pregunta de análisis de datos. Quiere ver que no asumes la causa.
▼
Tu respuesta
First, I segment before I conclude. Rising catalog + dropping activation almost always means a mix-shift problem: a few large suppliers are uploading high volumes while small suppliers are churning. The aggregate looks healthy but the cohort tells a different story.

I'd break activation rate by supplier size cohort, acquisition channel, and registration date. My hypothesis: there's a step in the onboarding funnel that works for large sophisticated suppliers — who can handle complex CSV formats or API integrations — but breaks for smaller, less technical ones. Then I look at drop-off points in the funnel by cohort to confirm where the divergence is.

The fix is almost always dual-track: a simpler guided path for the non-technical cohort, without removing the power-user path for enterprise.
Tip del entrevistador: «Segment before you conclude» es la respuesta correcta a cualquier diagnóstico de métricas en FYUL. Un aggregate que oculta una divergencia de cohorte es el error más común. Demostrar que lo ves antes de ser preguntado marca la diferencia.

17
"How do you define 'time-to-live' for a supplier catalog, and what levers would you pull to reduce it?" Definición precisa primero, luego palancas concretas. No al revés.
▼
Tu respuesta
Definition: Time-to-Live is the elapsed time from Decorator account creation to the first product being available for purchase by merchants. It's a wall-clock measure, not a processing-time measure — it includes everything the Decorator experiences, including wait times for async validations.

Three levers, ranked by impact:
  • Eliminate manual approval gates. Every step that requires a human at FYUL adds 4–24 hours of queue time. The goal is zero required approvals for standard submissions.
  • Real-time validation at submission. If the Decorator knows their data is invalid at the moment they upload — not 12 hours later — they fix it immediately. Moving validation from async to sync eliminates correction cycles.
  • Assisted catalog mapping. Auto-suggest field mappings based on the supplier's data structure. Reduces the time a Decorator spends figuring out how to format their catalog.
Tip del entrevistador: La distinción «wall-clock, no processing time» es técnicamente precisa y muestra que has medido esto en producción. El ranking de palancas por impacto, con la explicación del «por qué», es la respuesta que espera un Director de Producto, no solo un IC.
¿Cómo te salió?
18
"How do you set KPIs for initiatives where data quality is the main objective?" La calidad de datos es difusa si no la defines como métrica. Quiere precisión.
▼
Tu respuesta
Data quality is only useful as a KPI if it's tied to a user outcome. I use three layers:
  • Input quality: % of submitted products with all mandatory fields populated at first submission (without re-upload). Target: above 90%.
  • Processing quality: % of submitted SKUs that pass automated validation without human review. Target: above 95%.
  • Downstream quality: % of orders cancelled or reworked due to catalog data errors — because that's the merchant impact. A catalog that looks clean but generates 5% order errors is not clean.
The third metric is the one that matters most to the business and the one least teams track. Input quality is a leading indicator; order error rate is the lagging outcome.
Tip del entrevistador: La estructura leading vs. lagging indicator aplicada a calidad de datos es sofisticada. El tercer KPI — impacto en pedidos cancelados — conecta la calidad de datos con el P&L, que es lo que le importa a Marcel.

Colaboración y Manejo de Conflictos

19
"How do you align competing priorities between internal Operations (who want manual overrides) and Product (who want scalable automation)?" Conflicto real en supply platforms. Quiere pragmatismo, no purismo.
▼
Tu respuesta
I don't refuse manual overrides — I make them visible and temporary. In the short term, if an override unblocks a supplier that's waiting while we build the automated path, I allow it. But every override gets logged: which case, how often, and what it takes to resolve it manually.

That log becomes the automation roadmap. The most frequent manual overrides are the first features I build. Over time, the override rate becomes a lagging indicator of automation debt — if it's rising, we're not closing the loop fast enough.

The goal isn't zero human intervention ever — it's zero routine human intervention. Ops should be handling edge cases, not the default flow.
Tip del entrevistador: «Every override gets logged and becomes the automation roadmap» es exactamente el loop que FYUL quiere cerrar. No dices no a ops; los conviertes en tu principal fuente de datos sobre qué automatizar.

Tu respuesta
I launched a bulk catalog upload tool targeting enterprise suppliers, expecting it to reduce Time-to-Live for that cohort. The outcome: activation time for enterprise suppliers didn't improve. It stayed flat.

The lesson: I had validated the hypothesis with ops interviews, but not with data on where time was actually spent. I now require quantitative drop-off data before committing to a solution.
